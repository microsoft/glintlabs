# Interpretation guardrails

Use these rules whenever turning survey analytics into People Science interpretation.

## Core principles

1. Start with the decision the user needs to make.
2. Separate observation from inference.
3. Prefer practical effect and confidence over statistical novelty.
4. Preserve privacy and dignity for employee groups.
5. Name uncertainty plainly.
6. Recommend follow-up only when it changes action.

## Language discipline

Use:

- "The results suggest..."
- "A plausible interpretation is..."
- "This pattern is consistent with..."
- "This should be treated as directional because..."

Avoid:

- "This proves..."
- "Employees feel..."
- "This group is the problem..."
- "Managers should fix..."
- "Attrition is caused by..."

## Correlations

Correlations can identify related experiences but do not establish causality. Interpret them as prioritization signals or hypotheses.

Good interpretation:

> Items related to confidence in leadership also move with intent-to-stay outcomes. This suggests leadership clarity may be an important area to explore in follow-up conversations.

Bad interpretation:

> Leadership clarity causes attrition.

## Factor analysis

Factor analysis can reveal potential constructs, but labels require domain judgment. Treat factor names as working hypotheses unless backed by item content and prior theory.

Check:

- number of respondents
- number of items
- cross-loadings
- weak loadings
- whether factors are interpretable

For filtered solutions, require at least the greater of 100 complete responses
or five complete responses per item. Factor numbers can rotate or reorder
between cuts, so do not compare identically numbered factors as though
measurement invariance has been established.

## Cycle comparisons

Before interpreting change:

- check population comparability
- check item wording and scale consistency
- distinguish small movement from meaningful movement
- avoid over-weighting one-cycle noise

## By-attribute analysis

Segment differences can point to targeted listening needs, but should not stigmatize groups.

Use group findings to ask:

- Is the pattern large enough to matter?
- Is the group size sufficient?
- Does the pattern replicate across related items?
- Could organizational context explain the difference?

## Attrition analysis

Attrition associations are sensitive and should be handled conservatively.

Do:

- describe risk patterns as associations
- connect to broader survey themes when consistent
- recommend ethical follow-up and systemic action

Do not:

- predict individual attrition
- identify specific employees
- frame groups as flight risks without caveats
- imply causality without stronger evidence
