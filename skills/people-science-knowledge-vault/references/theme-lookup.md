## Research Drop:  Diving into AI Transformation Profiles to Understand Unique Needs Across Employee Personas

`research-drop-4336280` | research-drop | reviewed

**Priority:** New catalog; before relevant original-vault resources.

**Source:** https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoftvivablog/research-drop-diving-into-ai-transformation-profiles-to-understand-unique-needs-/4336280

November 2024 publication from the April 2024 AI-readiness study, shared with the readiness eBook and profiles deck rather than independent replication. Latent profiles combine engagement, prior change experience, and AI sentiment. The baseline recorded a profile-analysis subset of n=1,389; the current prose does not restate that denominator. RIVA comparisons include AI users only, with small skeptic/pessimist subsets, and measure perceived individual benefits, not audited ROI.

**Reviewed asset scope:** Complete freshly retrieved public article body read, including substantive published method/item appendices where present. Figures, chart pixels, linked assets, audio, private decks, and notes were not reviewed in this pass. Current themes are grounded in body prose, not chart alt text. Prior figure review is historical only and is recorded in the audit; it is not refreshed coverage.

**Study-family links:** ai-readiness-april-2024

1. **Five profiles combine engagement, change, and AI sentiment** (method) - Latent profile analysis grouped respondents into Multipliers, Advocates, Persuadables, AI Skeptics, and Change Pessimists using engagement, prior change experiences, and AI sentiment. Change experience covers vision, communication, and competence.
   Topics: change-management, ai-adoption, engagement, methods. Evidence: `L6`, "latent profile analysis (LPA)".
2. **Profiles are sample-specific, not a workforce census** (finding) - More than 70% of this study fell into Multiplier or Advocate profiles. The author cautions that local culture and unmeasured experiences such as leadership support or job-security concerns may yield different profiles; the proportions are not universal.
   Topics: ai-adoption, change-management, methods. Evidence: `L16`, "profiles are specific to the study sample".
3. **Use frequency differs across profiles** (finding) - Multipliers were most likely to use AI frequently, with progressively lower typical use across Advocates and Persuadables; nonuse was most common among Skeptics and Change Pessimists. These are concurrent profile associations, not effects of a persona-targeted intervention.
   Topics: ai-adoption, change-management. Evidence: `L30`, "Usage frequency follows a consistent downward pattern".
4. **Measure experienced value as well as use** (finding) - RIVA captures recognized individual work benefits rather than activity alone. Users in the Multiplier and Advocate groups reported the highest value, followed by Persuadables; usage counts do not fully describe a transformation experience.
   Topics: ai-value, survey-design. Evidence: `L34`, "not just that people are using AI at work".
5. **User-only comparisons exclude many hesitant employees** (method) - Only 25% of Skeptics and 43% of Change Pessimists used AI and entered the value comparison. Their small selected user subsets must not represent all employees in those profiles; among these users, pessimists reported higher RIVA than skeptics.
   Topics: ai-value, methods. Evidence: `L35`, "Only a small subset of AI Skeptics".
6. **Task-speed benefits can be easier to recognize** (recommendation) - Less enthusiastic profiles more often recognized faster task completion than less tangible benefits such as reduced stress or better decisions. Role-relevant scenarios are recommended to make broader benefits understandable, not as a guaranteed route to financial return.
   Topics: ai-value, learning-enablement. Evidence: `L36`, "when the value becomes less tangible".
7. **AI skepticism and change fatigue need different support** (finding) - Nonuse can reflect doubts about the technology or overwhelm with organizational change. User-only value patterns also differ: skeptics more often cited simplifying complexity, while pessimists more often cited productivity and decisions; avoid treating all hesitation alike.
   Topics: change-management, ai-value. Evidence: `L37`, "their reasons for hesitation and the value they see in AI are not the same".
8. **Change conditions rather than assigning fixed identities** (recommendation) - The author treats profiles as changeable as environments and experiences evolve. Tailor support through sound change practices instead of using a current profile as an enduring employee label; this analysis does not track actual individual profile transitions.
   Topics: change-management, inclusion. Evidence: `L40`, "not static".
9. **Include every profile in designing the transformation** (recommendation) - Champions can energize adoption while hesitant employees can improve accessible training. Use clear communication and continuous listening so engagement and previous change experience, not only enthusiasm for AI, inform an inclusive rollout.
   Topics: employee-listening, inclusion, change-management. Evidence: `L41`, "all types of profiles can be an asset".

## Research Drop: Amplifying Clarity and Impact of AI Agents by Aligning on Desired Outcomes

`research-drop-4462903` | research-drop | reviewed

**Priority:** New catalog; before relevant original-vault resources.

**Source:** https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoftvivablog/research-drop-amplifying-clarity-and-impact-of-ai-agents-by-aligning-on-desired-/4462903

October 2025 article using the July 2025 Agentic Teaming & Trust survey of 1,800 global employees. Alignment groups are based on two self-report items about outcomes and human-versus-agent tasks, not assigned treatments. The prior figure review recorded subsets of 1,262 agent users, 1,008 multi-agent users, and 1,157 users with teams; those denominators are not re-inspected here. Findings are concurrent reported associations, not causal ROI. Wipro's published case is separate from survey data.

**Reviewed asset scope:** Complete freshly retrieved public article body read, including substantive published method/item appendices where present. Figures, chart pixels, linked assets, audio, private decks, and notes were not reviewed in this pass. Current themes are grounded in body prose, not chart alt text. Prior figure review is historical only and is recorded in the audit; it is not refreshed coverage.

**Study-family links:** agentic-teaming-trust-july-2025

1. **Plan desired outcomes alongside agent activities** (recommendation) - Agent planning should define what success changes, not merely which tasks to automate. Ask which outcomes matter and how agents fit the team's way of working; these are recommendations, not proof that discussion itself creates transformation.
   Topics: ai-strategy, ai-agents, action-taking. Evidence: `L7`, "not just the  activities".
2. **Alignment groups are concurrent survey responses** (method) - Groups were formed from favorability on team outcome alignment and understanding human-judgment versus agent-supported tasks. This design does not isolate an outcome-planning treatment or independently measure strategy maturity.
   Topics: methods, ai-agents. Evidence: `L24`, "Employees were grouped in this analysis".
3. **Outcome-aligned respondents use agents more frequently** (finding) - Employees aligned on both activities and outcomes were 1.9 times as likely to be high-frequency agent users as those aligned only on activities. Shared causes or reverse direction remain possible in the observational comparison.
   Topics: ai-adoption, ai-agents. Evidence: `L10`, "1.9x as likely to be high-frequency agentic AI users".
4. **Outcome alignment accompanies multi-agent clarity** (finding) - Aligned respondents described clearer agent capabilities, use cases, and role boundaries. The article proposes this could reduce fragmented experimentation when scaling; it does not establish that alignment caused the clarity.
   Topics: role-clarity, ai-agents. Evidence: `L11`, "maintain clear role boundaries".
5. **Assess collective work beyond personal productivity** (recommendation) - Consider collaboration effectiveness and agent evaluation as agents reshape team processes. Personal output alone may miss how coordination and human-agent work change.
   Topics: ai-value, ai-agents, survey-design. Evidence: `L14`, "moving beyond individual productivity".
6. **Alignment accompanies team orientation and pride** (finding) - Outcome-aligned teams more often saw agents as teammates and reported knowledge sharing, creative problem-solving, and pride in collective accomplishments. These perceived teaming associations are distinct from objective business impact.
   Topics: ai-value, ai-agents, connection. Evidence: `L15`, "stronger teaming behaviors".
7. **Wipro screens agents for purpose, value, and feasibility** (case-study) - The separate Wipro case describes intake criteria for intended outcomes, business value, strategic fit, technical feasibility, and measurable success. Shared criteria are intended to avoid duplication and guide allocation; the Microsoft survey is not Wipro evidence or an evaluation of that process.
   Topics: ai-strategy, ai-agents, ai-governance. Evidence: `L19`, "Agent Intake and Prioritization Approach".

## Research Drop: Designing AI Feedback Systems by Industry

`research-drop-4484484` | research-drop | reviewed

**Priority:** New catalog; before relevant original-vault resources.

**Source:** https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoftvivablog/research-drop-designing-ai-feedback-systems-by-industry/4484484

January 2026 article analyzing the July 2025 Agentic Teaming & Trust panel of 1,800 global employees across the listed industries and job levels. The baseline recorded 1,624 AI users in the cadence figure, not re-inspected here. Reported channels are utilized channels, not a tested effectiveness ranking or necessarily preferences. Cross-sectional feedback/use links leave direction unresolved; external employee-centricity statistics are separate research. Social feedback capture requires privacy and consent safeguards, not indiscriminate collection of conversations.

**Reviewed asset scope:** Complete freshly retrieved public article body read, including substantive published method/item appendices where present. Figures, chart pixels, linked assets, audio, private decks, and notes were not reviewed in this pass. Current themes are grounded in body prose, not chart alt text. Prior figure review is historical only and is recorded in the audit; it is not refreshed coverage.

**Study-family links:** agentic-teaming-trust-july-2025

1. **Feedback opportunities are uneven, especially for ICs** (finding) - Sixty-six percent overall felt they had adequate AI-feedback opportunities, falling to 52% among individual contributors. The 34% overall opportunity gap is a perceived availability measure, not a response rate.
   Topics: employee-listening, inclusion, ai-adoption. Evidence: `L6`, "that drops to 52%".
2. **Leader and contributor support perceptions diverge** (finding) - The article repeats the panel's 78% leader versus 50% IC support contrast. This enablement gap is distinct from feedback-channel availability and should not be hidden by organization-wide averages.
   Topics: leadership, ai-adoption, inclusion. Evidence: `L11`, "78% of leaders".
3. **Daily users report more regular feedback requests** (finding) - Daily versus monthly users reported quarterly company surveys at 93% versus 71%, monthly pulses at 83% versus 50%, and weekly in-product prompts at 53% versus 23%. The comparison does not establish whether listening increases use or frequent users receive more attention.
   Topics: employee-listening, ai-adoption, methods. Evidence: `L12`, "high frequency AI users are markedly more likely".
4. **Fit the channel mix to actual work** (recommendation) - Desk-based versus shift-based routines, risk, regulation, and communication norms differ. Build accessible, consistent ways to signal what works rather than impose one supposedly optimal channel on every industry.
   Topics: employee-listening, work-design. Evidence: `L18`, "Roles, risk profiles, and communication norms vary widely".
5. **Healthcare needs particular attention to access** (finding) - Healthcare respondents were closer to half reporting sufficient feedback opportunities, compared with about two-thirds in many industries. The article recommends shift-aware, clinical-workflow-aware, privacy-sensitive channels; explanations for the gap are not established.
   Topics: employee-listening, inclusion, privacy-confidentiality. Evidence: `L19`, "healthcare sits closer to half".
6. **Informal conversations and team meetings dominate usage** (finding) - The most-used channels were largely social across industries. This is an observed usage pattern, not proof that informal feedback is superior or a reason to abandon formal surveys.
   Topics: employee-listening, connection, feedback. Evidence: `L22`, "Informal conversations and team meetings rank #1 and #2".
7. **Social forums feature in several frontline-heavy sectors** (finding) - Internal forums appeared prominently in construction, healthcare, and transportation/travel/hospitality. Mobile reach between tasks is the author's workflow interpretation, not a tested explanation of channel preference.
   Topics: employee-listening, work-design. Evidence: `L24`, "Internal social forums".
8. **Pulses feature in manufacturing and travel-related work** (finding) - Pulses were prominent in manufacturing and transportation/travel/hospitality. Fit with short, shift-friendly, team-specific check-ins is offered as a potential explanation, not a demonstrated advantage in response quality or outcomes.
   Topics: employee-listening, survey-design. Evidence: `L25`, "Pulse surveys".
9. **Embedded prompts follow application-centered work** (finding) - In-product feedback was common in construction, financial/professional services, retail/food/beverage, and technology. Point-of-experience relevance is a practical interpretation rather than a causal ranking of tools.
   Topics: employee-listening, work-design. Evidence: `L26`, "In ‑ product feedback tools".
10. **Capture context, route responsibility, and close the loop** (recommendation) - Use lightweight, appropriately governed feedback capture tied to a task or tool, route actionable context to product/IT/HR owners, and show resulting decisions. This is an operating recommendation, not permission to collect private conversations without safeguards.
   Topics: employee-listening, action-taking, privacy-confidentiality. Evidence: `L33`, "Close the loop  visibly".
11. **Connect everyday nuance with survey memory** (recommendation) - Recurring surveys and pulses supply organizational memory, while team dialogue supplies context. Connect these signals rather than substituting occasional large surveys for all listening or treating informal comments as representative population evidence.
   Topics: employee-listening, survey-design. Evidence: `L34`, "Surveys and pulses provide the backbone of organizational memory".

## Research Drop: Empowering Managers for an AI-First Future

`research-drop-4468191` | research-drop | reviewed

**Priority:** New catalog; before relevant original-vault resources.

**Source:** https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoftvivablog/research-drop-empowering-managers-for-an-ai-first-future/4468191

November 2025 article and public measurement appendix from the July 2025 Agentic Teaming & Trust panel of 1,800 global employees: 819 leaders, 520 managers, and 461 individual contributors. Findings are concurrent self-reports, not effects of training or manager role-modeling interventions. The appendix distinguishes critical thinking, trust, individual/team readiness, and individual/team perceived value; perceived value is not audited financial ROI.

**Reviewed asset scope:** Complete freshly retrieved public article body read, including substantive published method/item appendices where present. Figures, chart pixels, linked assets, audio, private decks, and notes were not reviewed in this pass. Current themes are grounded in body prose, not chart alt text. Prior figure review is historical only and is recorded in the audit; it is not refreshed coverage.

**Study-family links:** agentic-teaming-trust-july-2025

1. **Managers bridge strategy and team execution** (commentary) - The article argues for an active manager role in translating organizational AI strategy into team routines, use cases, and shared goals. This is the management rationale, not evidence that flattening structures necessarily succeeds or fails.
   Topics: manager-effectiveness, leadership, ai-strategy. Evidence: `L5`, "bridge between strategy and execution".
2. **Agent optimism coexists with manager strain** (finding) - Managers were broadly optimistic, yet half felt overwhelmed, 49% struggled with the pace, 55% reported at least two burnout indicators, and only 56% felt supported. Enthusiasm should not be mistaken for capacity to lead the change.
   Topics: manager-effectiveness, wellbeing, change-management. Evidence: `L14`, "only 56% feel they have the support".
3. **Shift from control toward curiosity and orchestration** (framework) - The proposed manager role emphasizes systems intelligence, role modeling, and orchestration rather than only control, know-how, and delegation. Resources and upskilling are prerequisites in the guidance, not measured outcomes of a redesign.
   Topics: manager-effectiveness, work-design, ai-agents. Evidence: `L17`, "from control to curiosity".
4. **Manager enablement is not yet a repeatable system** (finding) - Only 62% reported tools/resources and 60% opportunity; fewer than 30% attended AI training in six months. Prompt libraries or guides (20%) and AI coaching (19%) were also uncommon, motivating structured manager-specific support rather than ad-hoc resources.
   Topics: manager-effectiveness, learning-enablement, ai-skills. Evidence: `L25`, "less than 30% of managers have attended training".
5. **Make learning visible through trials and knowledge transfer** (recommendation) - Managers can use their knowledge of team workflows to surface use cases, run small experiments, share stories, and codify learning. The article recommends time, tools, reflection rituals, and recognition for insights as well as outcomes.
   Topics: manager-effectiveness, learning-enablement, feedback. Evidence: `L33`, "building reflection into team rituals".
6. **Role modeling accompanies value, critical thinking, and trust** (finding) - Employees reporting effective leader AI modeling also reported higher perceived agent value (+17 points), critical thinking (+22), and trust (+30). These are observational differences, not gains measured before and after a modeling intervention.
   Topics: leadership, ai-value, ai-skills, ai-governance. Evidence: `L29`, "For those who agree that their leaders role model".
7. **Psychological safety accompanies agent readiness and use** (finding) - Psychologically safe employees were 1.4 times as likely to report high-frequency agent use and reported greater readiness and perceived impact. The comparison does not establish that a safety intervention produced these outcomes.
   Topics: psychological-safety, ai-adoption, ai-agents. Evidence: `L36`, "1.4x as likely".
8. **Normalize human-agent roles and routine feedback** (recommendation) - Clarify human-versus-agent responsibilities and create regular opportunities for questions and shared learning. Only 41% said weekly team meetings asked about AI experience, motivating routine dialogue rather than assuming it already happens.
   Topics: role-clarity, employee-listening, psychological-safety. Evidence: `L37`, "only  41% of employees".
9. **Individual and team readiness are distinct constructs** (method) - The appendix measures individual understanding, motivation, skills, value, and opportunity; team readiness adds human/agent task boundaries, shared outcomes, openness, trust, and collective skills. One score should not stand in for all of these conditions.
   Topics: survey-design, ai-adoption, ai-agents. Evidence: `L63`, "Team Agent Readiness".
10. **Agent trust includes dependence, risk, and disclosure** (method) - The public trust items address willingness under uncertainty or risk, confidence and dependability, and sharing work difficulties with an agent. 1. **External norms orient interpretation rather than prescribe success** (framework) - Benchmarks aggregate anonymous cross-company scores to contextualize relative strengths and weaknesses; they are reference points, not definitions of what a particular organization must achieve.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-interpretation. Evidence: `p3`, "Benchmarks are aggregated external reference".
2. **Preserve exact standard-item wording for comparability** (recommendation) - Use standard questions as written when external comparison is needed, because wording changes can affect results. Choose content for strategic relevance rather than maximizing the number of benchmarked items.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-design. Evidence: `p5`, "questions exactly as written".
3. **Percentile benchmarks are item-level entry thresholds** (documentation) - High-performing top-10% or top-25% norms identify the threshold score for that item, not the mean of a stable group of top companies across every question.
   Topics: benchmarks, methods. Evidence: `p6`, "threshold score to be in the top tier".
4. **Suite availability follows population and data sufficiency** (documentation) - Global, industry, country/region, lifecycle, and specialized suites answer different population questions. Lifecycle items are included where data suffices, and specialty suites are available and renewed only with sufficient data.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-design. Evidence: `p6`, "Only available and renewed if data is sufficient".
5. **Industry labels can conceal larger within-industry differences** (recommendation) - The guide cautions that engagement often varies more within industries than between them, industry labels miss organizational diversity, and talent markets cross industries. A narrower sector norm is not automatically more applicable.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-interpretation, methods. Evidence: `p6`, "more variation within industries than".
6. **Function-specific and custom cuts are not supported** (documentation) - Inconsistent definitions of functions make comparable functional benchmarks impractical. The guide also declines custom cuts because narrow samples are less robust, costly, and often less useful than broad comparisons.
   Topics: benchmarks, methods. Evidence: `p6`, "does not offer function-".
7. **Triangulate external, internal, and historical comparisons** (framework) - External norms supply market context, internal comparisons reveal relative team patterns, and historical scores track an organization's own progress. Use the three together rather than treating one lens as a complete explanation or causal evaluation.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-interpretation. Evidence: `p11`, "richest insights come from seeing".
8. **Broad norms usually provide a stronger reference** (recommendation) - Prefer global or high-performing norms for broad talent markets and complex organizations. Narrow cuts need a strategic reason and may rest on fewer companies; specificity alone is not quality.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-interpretation. Evidence: `p13`, "a broader".
9. **Geographic response tendencies warrant contextual checks** (recommendation) - Country or regional norms can contextualize differences potentially influenced by response culture. Compare relevant populations and avoid inferring that a global gap necessarily signals a local problem; local norms supplement rather than replace broader context.
   Topics: benchmarks, inclusion, survey-interpretation. Evidence: `p13`, "cultural response tendencies".
10. **Custom questions can be useful without external norms** (recommendation) - Survey strategically important unique topics even when no external benchmark exists; interpret them using internal trends, group comparisons, and qualitative follow-up instead of omitting them.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-design, survey-interpretation. Evidence: `p14`, "that shouldn’t deter you from asking".
11. **Explain benchmark meaning and confidentiality to stakeholders** (recommendation) - Describe benchmarks as anonymous aggregates, calibrate unrealistic executive expectations, and keep employee experience and business outcomes as the objective rather than chasing a reference score.
   Topics: benchmarks, privacy-confidentiality, leadership. Evidence: `p14`, "not a prescriptive target".
12. **Investigate gaps and preserve strengths before choosing action** (recommendation) - A large gap can reflect composition, a temporary situation, a strategic trade-off, or a genuine opportunity. Explore why, then balance closing relevant gaps with sustaining and sharing strengths; the examples do not identify a local root cause.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-interpretation, action-taking. Evidence: `p15`, "Translate benchmark gaps into action thoughtfully".
13. **Set directional behavior improvements rather than score targets** (recommendation) - Prioritize incremental, business-linked behavior changes rather than benchmark-based numeric targets that may be unachievable or encourage unhealthy score-chasing.
   Topics: benchmarks, action-taking. Evidence: `p15`, "Avoid setting targets".
14. **Aggregation gives each customer an item score** (method) - The described pipeline aggregates at survey-cycle level, averages the customer's surveys into one score per item, then averages item scores across customers. It is not a pooled average giving every employee response equal weight.
   Topics: benchmarks, methods. Evidence: `p17`, "aggregated in three stages".
15. **Representativeness concerns organizations rather than raw response totals** (method) - The guide emphasizes number, diversity, and representativeness of participating organizations; its weighting description says organizations in a size category are weighted alike. A headline respondent total alone does not describe the effective item-level evidence base.
   Topics: benchmarks, methods. Evidence: `p17`, "number, diversity,".
16. **Item-usage audits determine coverage** (method) - Twice-yearly usage audits determine which questions have sufficient data; the guide reports more than 175 benchmarked items. It does not publish an item-specific universal minimum in this text.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-design, methods. Evidence: `p18`, "item usage audit twice".
17. **Customer-only norms have a new-content seeding exception** (method) - Standard norms have used customer scores since early 2021. The guide notes a rare exception for seeding brand-new content with panel data, replaced when sufficient client data becomes available.
   Topics: benchmarks, methods. Evidence: `p18`, "initial seeding of brand-new content".
18. **Rolling windows balance stability and recency** (documentation) - The guide uses a rolling 12-month window and describes semiannual global/top-percentile updates with most industry/country updates annual. This is refresh methodology, not proof that successive versions form an unchanged longitudinal sample.
   Topics: benchmarks, methods. Evidence: `p20`, "Rolling 12-Month Data Window".
19. **Report interpretation depends on the active comparator and vintage** (documentation) - Users may switch comparison groups; suite names and years identify the external norm, while deltas, detailed values, heatmaps, and exports reflect the selected reference. The displayed score examples are illustrations, not customer findings.
   Topics: benchmarks, survey-interpretation, glint-admin. Evidence: `p21`, "Report Settings".
20. **External benchmark access requires organizational opt-in** (documentation) - The guide links administrator consent for anonymous external processing with benchmark visibility. Without opt-in, users see internal comparators; admins should select a sensible default and orient managers to switching views.
   Topics: benchmarks, privacy-confidentiality, glint-admin. Evidence: `p23`, "must opt-in to use external benchmarks".
